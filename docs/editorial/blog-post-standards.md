# Blog post standards

Use this guide when drafting or revising posts for `content/blog/`. Read the author's supplied draft and source material first. These are editorial defaults, not a template: the subject and Aaron's own account decide the structure. Follow the repository's publication and content rules in `CLAUDE.md`, `GEMINI.md`, and `docs/AGENT_ROUTING.md`.

## Write a story about the work

- Start with a specific idea, decision, question, scene, or result that gives a reader a reason to continue. A deadline or a generic warning about AI is rarely a strong hook on its own.
- Show what Aaron first thought, what challenged it, what he checked, and what changed. Keep the causal links visible. A post about research should say what the research question is and why it matters to someone who might use the result.
- Make the post sound like Aaron: first person when the experience is his, reflective, candid about mistakes, and curious about other people's experience. Use his actual examples and wording as the anchor. Do not manufacture personal scenes, feelings, conversations, credentials, or outcomes; leave a short question for him when a detail is missing.
- Give the reader a clear path through the work. If the post covers brainstorming, review, evidence, and presentation, explain how one stage changed the next. Show what happened after a presentation or release when that is part of the story.

## Keep it accessible without making it vague

- Assume an interested reader who may use consumer AI apps but does not know the project's files, developer tools, or research shorthand. Explain a technical concept through the decision or problem it helps address; define essential terms on first use.
- Keep a few concrete technical details when they reveal what made the idea difficult or interesting. Do not replace a specific proposal with “a model” or a real source check with “we verified it.” Remove implementation mechanics, file histories, and tool inventories that do not help the reader understand the story.
- Name tools only when their distinct roles matter. Describe what each contributed in ordinary language: brainstorming, reading sources, challenging claims, or reviewing a draft cold. Avoid unsupported claims about model internals or universal AI behavior.
- Prefer examples, choices, and consequences over abstract lessons. Use a heading to advance the story, not to label every stage of an internal workflow.

## Be precise about evidence and limits

- Check material claims, references, and numbers against sources that support the exact wording. Distinguish a search finding from proof that no prior work exists, and a promising case from a general result. Do not treat an assistant's “verified” label as independent evidence.
- Keep Aaron's judgement separate from what a source established. Say when a change came from critique, a checked fact, or his own reasoning. Report failures as part of the process and explain what changed in response.
- State material limits without turning the post into a disclaimer: one project is one project; a talk that explored framing did not necessarily test the method; unmeasured time or cost cannot support efficiency claims. Preserve the author's own assessment of what was useful.
- Keep private contacts, credentials, and unpublished or copyrighted source material out of public drafts. Do not invent citations or quote source papers beyond what the assignment allows.

## Edit for a reader

- Read the whole piece for its main point before line editing. Keep the best example of a point and cut repetitions, generic setup, tool name lists, and detours that do not move the story forward. Do not cut the detail that makes the post distinctive.
- Check that the title and opening promise the same subject the body delivers. Keep transitions clear, paragraphs focused, and language plain without flattening Aaron's voice into a tutorial or product pitch.
- End with what the experience suggests and, when appropriate, a real question that invites peers to compare their experience. Avoid claiming the final proposal is correct merely because the process was helpful.
- For a website post, follow nearby real `content/blog/` pages for frontmatter and page assets. Keep new posts in draft status until publication is requested. A final editorial pass prepares copy for Aaron's review; it does not authorize posting it.

## Before handing over a draft

1. Can a new reader say what Aaron was trying to do, what changed his mind, and why?
2. Are the most interesting details concrete and understandable without prior project context?
3. Are important claims and numbers supported at the level stated?
4. Does each section earn its space, and have repeated explanations been cut?
5. Are unresolved facts presented as questions for Aaron rather than filled in by the agent?
