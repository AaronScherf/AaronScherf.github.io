# From USAID to Columbia: Sharing My Research and the Tools Behind It

*A new website for research on disasters, conflict, and recovery, alongside practical software others can use.*

This fall, I started a PhD in Sustainable Development at Columbia University. My goals remain focused on supporting international development and poverty alleviation in the most vulnerable communities, but I believe my greatest impact can come through applied research and advancing critical new ideas rather than directly implementing development programs. As part of my commitment to open-source and reproducible research, I've also launched [my personal website](https://aaronscherf.github.io/), where I'll share the research questions I'm pursuing, the software and data analysis tools I'm building, and what I learn along the way.

Before Columbia, I worked as a Foreign Service Officer with USAID, including on program design and social inclusion in Colombia and monitoring and evaluation in wartime Ukraine. Those experiences taught me that the gap between researchers and practitioners is most acute in complex settings, particularly areas affected by conflict and vulnerable to climate disasters, where conditions change quickly and reliable information is hard to obtain. Effective social protections and development programs depend on implementing the "best practices" built on detailed evidence and tacit experience, but how can governments or humanitarian organizations synthesize disparate sources for totally novel situations? If a complex crisis requires a rapid response, there is no time to conduct detailed analyses, responders have to act with whatever tools are available. If the context itself makes it difficult to monitor progress or evaluate success, then those same implementers need whatever metrics are available to adapt to changing facts on the ground. Researchers have often been hesitant to engage on these topics precisely because the available data is lacking, but the frequency and scale of conflicts and disasters are only increasing, so the need for novel approaches is acute.

My research will focus on this gap: how communities and humanitarian organizations prepare for and recover from overlapping climate disasters, armed conflict, and economic shocks. I'm interested in the decisions that help people protect their livelihoods, avoid displacement, and rebuild over the longer term.

One of my main questions is to what extent approaches used in natural disaster response, which have been thoroughly studied, can be adapted to conflict settings or complex crises. Some topics I hope to explore include index insurance, with payouts linked to measurable conditions such as rainfall or conflict severity, and cash assistance delivered digitally to vulnerable communities before a crisis escalates. I want to understand when these tools help, the best way to design and finance them, how different types of conflict changes their effectiveness, and which local conditions explain differences in outcomes.

My initial research plans include studies of Somalia and Ukraine, bringing together satellite observations, conflict records, market prices, and displacement data. I'll draw on economics, climate science, and machine learning, alongside my experience directly implementing and evaluating development programs. My goal is to produce evidence that can help practitioners plan and evaluate humanitarian and social protection programs in uncertain environments, with reproducible tools and methodologies that can be applied in a variety of settings.

My website connects that future work with projects I've already undertaken. My [wheat-yield research](https://aaronscherf.github.io/projects/wheat_yield_prediction/) thesis from UC Berkeley compared satellite observations, experimental data, and prediction models to validate the extension of farm trials to novel contexts. More recently, I've been building a few side projects to explore new methods of AI-assisted coding, via the [Academic Hub](https://aaronscherf.github.io/projects/academic-hub-overview/) and an [AI Research Assistant](https://aaronscherf.github.io/projects/ai-research-assistant-overview/): Python and LLM-based tools for transcribing and organizing qualitative technical information, finding relevant academic literature, and synthesizing complex information sources to inform credible summaries and databases.

I'll post regularly on [my website's blog](https://aaronscherf.github.io/blog/) and [Medium](https://aaronscherf.medium.com/) as the work develops, sharing research updates, software projects, and practical explanations of my methods. Some posts will explore substantive questions about disasters or recovery; others will walk through a particular tool or a lesson from building it. My hope is that development practitioners, researchers, and students will each find something they can use.

Making the work reusable is part of the plan. I'll share open-source code, documentation, and examples on my [website](https://aaronscherf.github.io/) and [GitHub](https://github.com/AaronScherf), with clear information about the data and setup needed to reproduce an analysis or adapt a tool. Where source data cannot be redistributed, I'll explain the requirements and provide public or sample inputs where possible.

Even the [website's source and setup](https://github.com/AaronScherf/AaronScherf.github.io) are available for others to adapt. It builds on the open-source HugoBlox Academic CV template, so you can use the same foundation for your own research or project portfolio.

I'd welcome conversations with researchers working on related questions and organizations looking for help with program evaluation, data analysis, or research tools. To my former USAID colleagues and partners, I also hope this offers an easy way to stay in touch and exchange ideas on how to continue advancing our mission.

Please reach out to me via [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/aaron-scherf) or [email](mailto:theaaronscherf@gmail.com) if you'd like to collaborate or discuss further. I'd especially like to hear from practitioners focused on development, humanitarian, or disaster response to learn which tools might be useful in your work.
